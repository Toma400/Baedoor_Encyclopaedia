## Letters
One of the most prominents changes of Third Era is removal of t-related double consonants, which
made big impact on writing at the time, and became main concern between third and fourth era.

|                    |                  Letters                   |
|--------------------|:------------------------------------------:|
| Doubled Consonants | th, gh, sh, oe, oa, ar, ah, at, ŝh, ya, ye |
| Early Consonants   |                    r, n                    |
| Late Consonants    |              y, d, u, m, t, p              |
| Vowels             |                 e, o, a, s                 |

## Pronunciation Rules

<span style="color:#347DD5">**OE**</span>  
*OE* has specific pronunciation difference when there's vowel preceding it in a word. In such
case, it should be read as rather `e` instead of `oe`.  
This can be nicely shown in word such as *Ta'hra'oe's*, which is read as `ta:hra:ees`.

<span style="color:#347DD5">**GH / AH**</span>  
If `gh` and `ah` are followed by late consonant (y, d, u, m), the pronunciation of those changes
to, respectively, `k↓f` and `aih`.  
Additionally, other use of `gh` sounds more like `kh` from now on, making it a bit softer.

<span style="color:#347DD5">**SH / ŜH**</span>  
`Sh` and new `Ŝh` were another consonants getting their rules changed when followed by late consonant
(being a star `*` in example):
- `Sh*`  - pronounced as `ʃ`
- `Sh'*` - pronounced as `ʃh`
- `Ŝh`   - pronounced as `shi`
- `Ŝh*`  - pronounced as `ʃi`
- `Ŝh'*` - pronounced as `ʃhi`

<span style="color:#347DD5">**Y**</span>  
In two cases, `y'` changes its usual pronunciation, being `ii`:
1. if the word ends with it; it is then pronounced as `ij` (for example in `tar'ay'`)
2. if the word has consonant after it; it is then pronounced as `ji`

Additionally, i-derivatives use the same pronunciation key, so `ya` and `ye` are pronounced as
`ja` and `je` (`j` as yod).

<span style="color:#347DD5">**T' / P'**</span>  
These two new consonants have also special treatment on Third Era, because in this period one
could extend those letters, as well as "u".  
In `t'` and `p'` case though, they changed their pronunciation if there was consonant following, or
the word ended on this particular word. In such case, the pronunciation changed, respectively, to
`f` and single `p`. Those extensions were not liked nor popular, nevertheless.

<span style="color:#347DD5">**D**</span>  
`D` letter sometimes is read as "th", but this was rather a thing used by dialects.

## Changes
Third Era pushed smoothness of the language further, adding next pronunciation rules and
three more consonants. Weirdly, it also marked temporary end of t-related consonants in favour of
single `t`, making it closer to other languages at the time.
